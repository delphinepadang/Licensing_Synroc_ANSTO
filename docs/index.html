01 · The problem

A proven fix,
an unknown market

Making molybdenum-99 (Mo-99) leaves 2.6–15 m³ of liquid radioactive waste a year worldwide [1], [3]. Cemented, it grows about 25-fold and is stored for decades until a repository opens [4]. ANSTO's Synroc shrinks it instead, and its first plant, SyMo, is being commissioned [5]. What ANSTO lacks is an evidence-based way to decide where to offer it.

What changes

Cemented: 25 m³ Synroc: 0.25–1 m³

Cemented, each cubic metre becomes about 25 m³ of packages that must be stored, and paid for, until disposal. Synroc turns it into 0.25–1 m³ of dense ceramic [2], [3].

Our aim: give ANSTO an evidence-based answer, and a reusable tool, for where and in what commercial form to offer Synroc abroad.

Who decides whether it works

Waste owners: does it save money?
Regulators: can it be licensed?
Scientists: does the waste match?
Host towns: will they accept it?

Why ANSTO cannot just pick a country

A market exists only where all four conditions hold at once. Judged on any one alone, the obvious first market changes:

Biggest inventory → Canada
Readiest regulator → United Kingdom
Matching chemistry → every fission Mo-99 producer
Future supply → France

So we did not start from the Netherlands and Belgium. We started from eight candidates, tested every one on all four conditions, and let the evidence choose. The two European sites are the result of the screen, not its starting point, and the same tool can re-test any country as evidence changes.

02 · Where the waste is

Eight candidates, each a plausible first market

We searched the Commonwealth and the EU because their rules differ: the EU has a binding waste directive [23]; the Commonwealth has none. Inside them we took every fission Mo-99 producer, plus each country a one-condition search would have picked, the EU's shared rules, and Australia as benchmark. Cases likely to fail were included on purpose: a screen that passed everyone would prove nothing, and each exclusion tells ANSTO where not to spend effort. Click a country.

No jurisdiction legally blocks Synroc. Legal score: how clear the licensing pathway is. Political score: Kingdon's policy-window model [7]. Both 1–5.

03 · Four questions

Four questions, one decision

Every candidate faces the same four questions, one per condition a market must meet. Three answers can rule a country out at once (no matching waste, a legal block, too little waste to pay); the rest become scores in the decision tool.

Why the Netherlands ranks first, and how we tested it

1 · It is never worse. The Netherlands scores 3, 5, 4 and 3 on the technical, economic, law-and-politics and social criteria; Belgium scores 2, 4, 3.5 and 3. With weights w summing to one, the gap is

V(NL) − V(BE) = wtech + wecon + 0.5 wlaw

This is above zero for every weighting except one that puts all weight on social licence, where the two tie. In MCDA terms, the Netherlands dominates Belgium [6].

2 · Random weights (SMAA-2). ANSTO has not set weights, so we drew 10,000 random sets, every combination equally likely, and recorded who ranked first [11]. The chart shows the gap in every draw: all are above zero.

3 · Uncertain scores. Scores are judgements, so we also let every score move by up to half a point, then a full point, in 10,000 more draws each.

4 · The one weak point. The ranking does not depend on preferences, but it does depend on one fact: Petten's waste volume, which is estimated at 0.7 m³ a year. Measuring it is the first task.

05 · The economics

Timing, not chemistry, decides

Where the waste agency charges for disposal at hand-over, every cubic metre avoided saves the full charge. Where disposal is decades away, discounting erases the saving.

06 · What could go wrong

Sixteen risks, three that could stop everything

Agency refusal (R1), no operating licence for SyMo (R6) and a formulation that fails on real waste (R7) would remove both countries at once. They cannot be diversified, so each becomes a gate. Ratings follow ISO 31000; the full register is in the report, Appendix A.

07 · Our plan

Our recommendation

We recommend licensing Synroc to COVRA (Netherlands) first and ONDRAF (Belgium) in parallel, each priced on the disposal cost the agency avoids, with no licence signed until three gates are passed. Click a step.

How ANSTO engages the Netherlands and Belgium

Government first. Brief DFAT and use the Australia–Euratom nuclear cooperation agreement to open talks in The Hague and Brussels.
Waste agencies. Propose joint waste characterisation and Synroc tests with COVRA and ONDRAF.
Producers and regulators. Confirm volumes with Curium and IRE (Petten's has never been published); brief ANVS and FANC early.
Communities. Each licensee consults its host community (Petten or Nieuwdorp, Fleurus or Dessel) before any contract.

Limitations. Public documents only; no interviews with COVRA, ONDRAF or the producers. The Dutch volume is estimated. TRL 6 is provisional. Social licence evidence cannot yet compare sites. The eight cases test the framework rather than cover every country; producers outside the two blocs were not assessed, but the tool can screen them.
